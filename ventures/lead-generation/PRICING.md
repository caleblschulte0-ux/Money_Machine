# Pricing

| Model | Price |
| --- | --- |
| Pay per lead (roofing, exclusive) | $85 |
| Exclusive monthly territory | $2,500/month |
| Appointment fee | planned, not priced |
| Revenue share | not offered |

## Reasoning

**$85 per exclusive roofing lead** sits within the range regional contractors
already pay for shared leads, and the exclusivity is the differentiator rather
than the price. Competing on price in lead generation is a race to the bottom
against companies with far more ad budget.

**$2,500/month for territory** is roughly 30 leads at the per-lead rate,
discounted for commitment and predictability. It is the model to move buyers
toward: predictable revenue, and a buyer invested in working the leads properly.

## What is not offered

**Revenue share.** It requires visibility into the buyer's closed jobs, which
they will not give accurately, and every settlement becomes an argument. Simple
pricing that both sides can verify is worth more than theoretically better
pricing that neither can.

**Non-exclusive discounts.** Selling the same lead to several buyers is the
single fastest way to destroy the business.

**Volume commitments at a discount.** We do not control volume and will not
price as though we do.

## The constraint that governs everything

Cost per qualified lead must stay meaningfully below $85. Above it for two
consecutive months is a kill criterion, and no amount of volume fixes it.

The buyer's economics set the ceiling on price: a roofing contractor closing
20% of leads at a $12,000 average job earns roughly $2,400 of revenue per lead
delivered. $85 is defensible arithmetic at that close rate — and the moment we
claim a close rate rather than cite theirs, it becomes a promise we cannot keep.

## Credits, not refunds

An accepted dispute issues a credit against future leads rather than a cash
refund. Credits keep the relationship going; refunds end it. Cash refunds are
available on request, because refusing them buys a chargeback.
